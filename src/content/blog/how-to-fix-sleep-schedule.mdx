If your bedtime has drifted later, start with a schedule you can actually keep. Choose a wake time, make room for **at least seven hours of sleep** if you are an adult, and use light and regular habits to help your body adjust. A single late night is different from a persistent sleep problem: you do not need to “reset” your body after every imperfect night.

This guide is for adults whose sleep timing has slipped because of travel, late nights, or irregular routines. If you regularly cannot fall asleep despite enough opportunity, work rotating shifts, or feel sleepy while driving, the [when to seek help](#when-to-seek-help) section matters more than a do-it-yourself schedule.

## Start with a realistic target

Write down four things before changing your routine:

1. **Your current pattern:** approximate bedtime and wake time over the last week. Use your actual times, including weekends.
2. **Your required wake time:** the time you need to get up most days.
3. **Your sleep opportunity:** a bedtime that leaves at least seven hours for sleep, plus some time to fall asleep. Seven hours is an adult minimum, not a promise that seven hours will be enough for you.
4. **Your biggest obstacle:** late work, caregiving, light, caffeine, stress, or something else you can address.

For example, if you need to wake at 7:00 a.m. and usually take about 30 minutes to fall asleep, a midnight bedtime leaves only 6.5 hours of possible sleep. Planning an earlier bedtime matters more than trying to wake at the end of a theoretical 90-minute cycle. Our [bedtime calculator](/) can show possible times, but it cannot measure your sleep stages or tell you exactly when you will fall asleep.

The [CDC says adults ages 18–60 need seven or more hours of sleep](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html). Children and teenagers need more; this plan is not a pediatric schedule.

## A practical seven-day reset

There is no universal number of days to change a body clock. This example moves an 8:00 a.m. wake time toward 7:00 a.m. while preserving time for sleep. Adjust the size and pace of the shift to what you can sustain.

- **Days 1–2:** Wake at 7:45 a.m. Get outside or into bright daylight after waking. Begin winding down early enough to allow a full night of sleep.
- **Days 3–4:** Wake at 7:30 a.m. Keep the morning light routine. Dim bright indoor lighting near bedtime and put away activities that keep you alert.
- **Days 5–6:** Wake at 7:15 a.m. Keep meal, activity, and sleep timing as regular as your life allows.
- **Day 7 and after:** Try 7:00 a.m. if you are getting enough sleep and functioning well during the day. If you are accumulating sleep loss, slow down and make more room for sleep.

These 15-minute steps are a planning example, not a medical treatment or a guaranteed pace. A consistent schedule can help, but forcing an earlier alarm while leaving bedtime unchanged simply reduces sleep. The [NIH recommends regular sleep and wake times, including weekends](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation/healthy-sleep-habits).

## Use light at the right part of the day

Light helps synchronize the sleep-wake cycle. Try to get daylight during the day, especially after waking, and reduce bright light close to bedtime. The [NIH explains how light and caffeine affect the sleep-wake cycle](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep/sleep-wake-cycle). You do not need a special lamp for a basic routine.

Timing can be more complicated if you work nights, travel across time zones, or have a circadian rhythm disorder. Bright-light devices and melatonin are treatments that depend on the condition and timing; using them at the wrong time may work against your goal. The [NIH advises working with a healthcare professional on circadian-rhythm treatments](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/circadian-rhythm-disorders/treatment). This guide does not prescribe a light-box schedule or melatonin dose.

## Make the evening easier to repeat

Choose a wind-down routine you can do most nights, even when the day is busy. Dim the lights, put tomorrow's tasks somewhere other than your head, and leave enough time for sleep. A calm activity such as reading may help you transition, but you do not need a perfect ritual.

Check caffeine timing. Caffeine can interfere with sleep for hours, and sensitivity varies. If sleep is difficult, move your last caffeinated drink earlier and observe whether it helps. The [NIH recommends avoiding caffeine close to bedtime](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation/healthy-sleep-habits). Our [caffeine cutoff tool](/calculators/caffeine-cutoff) gives a planning estimate, not a personal metabolism test.

Keep the room dark, quiet, and comfortable if you can. If a partner's schedule, childcare, or your job makes a fixed bedtime unrealistic, focus first on a workable sleep opportunity and the parts of the routine you control.

## What if you miss a night?

Resume your usual routine without turning one late night into a week of very short nights. If you are dangerously sleepy, safety comes first: do not drive while drowsy. A short nap may help some people, but naps late in the day can make it harder to fall asleep at night. The [NIH healthy sleep habits guide](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation/healthy-sleep-habits) discusses naps and daytime habits.

Track the pattern for a week: when you got into bed, roughly when you slept, when you woke, and how alert you felt. A [sleep journal](/tools/sleep-journal) can help you spot repeatable obstacles. Focus on the trend rather than the score from any one night.

## When to seek help

A schedule change is unlikely to solve every cause of tiredness. Talk with a healthcare professional if you regularly cannot sleep despite sufficient opportunity, have loud snoring or breathing pauses, feel excessive daytime sleepiness, or your work schedule repeatedly conflicts with sleep. The [NIH describes how circadian rhythm disorders are evaluated](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/circadian-rhythm-disorders/diagnosis). Treatment for persistent insomnia or a circadian disorder can be more specific than general sleep-habit advice.

## The short version

Pick a sustainable wake time. Protect enough time for sleep. Get daylight during your day, reduce bright light late in your day, and keep a routine you can repeat. Change the schedule gradually if that makes it easier to maintain. If sleep remains difficult or daytime sleepiness affects safety, get clinical help rather than shortening your night further.
